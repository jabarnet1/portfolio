Personal Project · Learning LangGraph

Teaching myself agentic AI by solving a problem that actually mattered

My spouse's café, Libre Dessert, is expanding into catering — boardroom breakfasts and lunches for corporate campuses, medical practices, and office complexes near Morristown, Newton, and Rockaway, NJ. I used it as my first real LangGraph build.

LiBre Dessert Café
The situation

Six hours a week, done by hand

Finding, researching, and qualifying catering prospects the manual way — Google Maps, clicking through websites, guessing at headcounts, copying into a spreadsheet — was costing about six hours a week before a single sales conversation happened.

Why not just ChatGPT?

It forgets between sessions, so nothing tracks who's already been contacted. It can't be trusted with facts — ask for a headcount and you get a confident number that might be invented, with no way to tell. It doesn't do anything — no database, no email, no calendar. And it doesn't scale past a conversation: 200 businesses means 200 prompts, pasted by hand. The goal wasn't "use AI" — it was build a system that uses AI for what it's actually good at, and lets normal software handle memory, records, and action.

Parallelism

Send fan-out

Researching 200 businesses sequentially takes ~13 minutes. Fanning them out in parallel with LangGraph's Send takes about 20 seconds — same work, same accuracy.

Human-in-the-loop

interrupt()

This is what separates a script from a supervisable system. A script runs to completion or dies. A checkpointed graph can pause for a person's judgment and resume exactly where it left off — even on a different server, days later.

The insight that mattered more than any code
buyer_is_third_party

In the medical segment, the buyer often isn't the practice — pharmaceutical reps buy lunch for medical offices constantly, on a recurring, budgeted basis with a per-head cap. Different buyer, different trigger, different pitch, different lead source. That fact only mattered once it became a field the system could act on. The gap between a generic CRM and a useful one is entirely made of fields like that one — and finding them is a business-understanding skill, not a technical one.

Engineering discipline that carried over
What I held myself to

None of this is exotic — it's the same discipline that makes any system trustworthy, applied to an AI pipeline instead of traditional software:

Structural safety, not conditional — the demo can't email a real business because it has no mailbox, not because a flag says don't
Architectural rules enforced by CI, not just written in a README
Every AI-derived fact keeps its source, timestamp, and raw original
Honest "unknown" beats a confident, fabricated number — tested on purpose with sparse fixtures
Reproduce a bug in isolation before attempting a fix
Dev and production stay identical on purpose — every difference is a place bugs hide
