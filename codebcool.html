Volunteer Teaching · Kids & Code

Teaching kids to think like programmers, one analogy at a time

Two seasons, twenty sessions, one free platform — from "what does a computer actually do" to shipping a playable game, using nothing but analogies from the kids' own lives.

The program

CodeBCool

A volunteer coding program for kids, built entirely around MIT Scratch. Season 1 ran biweekly from late 2018 through mid-2019; Season 2 picked up in January 2020, fully virtual, and closed that June. Every session followed the same rule: a real-world analogy first, the programming concept second, the Scratch blocks last.

The teaching philosophy

Make the absence of the idea painful, then hand over the fix

Every abstraction in the curriculum arrives as relief, not as a rule to memorize.

"
The core teaching move
Manufactured Tedium

To teach loops: have the kids write out MyLap = 1 through MyLap = 5 by hand, then push to 200 laps until the slide itself gives up — "I am getting tired of typing…" The loop shows up right after, as the answer to a problem they just felt themselves. The concept never arrives as a rule. It arrives as relief.

Pattern

Dual Analogies

Every abstraction gets two unrelated concrete anchors. Input/Process/Output is clay, a pizza, and a smoothie blender — in the same session. If one door doesn't open, another does.

Pattern

Personalization

Examples pulled from the kids' own lives — their names, their pets, their allowance. Season 2 formalized it: every student got a different variant of the same exercise, tuned to their own interests.

Pattern

Spiral Review

Every major concept returns later, harder, never verbatim. Functions get taught twice a season. The four-corners exercise becomes the four-edges exercise. Conditionals come back nested.

Standout moments

A few that stuck

01 · The Hook

"Where Is the Process?"

Session 1 overlays Input, Memory, and Output directly onto a screenshot of the Scratch editor — then asks where the process lives, and doesn't answer. The same slide reappears at the end of the session, and again to open Season 2 a year later. The question is left in the room to be earned, not told.

02 · The Analogy

The Nerf Gun

Prepare bullets, pump, aim, shoot — with a loop-back arrow from the last step to the first. Four photos, no code, and it captures named sequence, order, and reusability in one image built from something every kid in the room had already used that week. It got re-run, verbatim, three separate times across both seasons.

03 · The Trick

Vocabulary as Payload

The nested if/else lab in Season 2 outputs words like SCRUMPTIOUS, GOBBLEDYGOOK, and SEPTUAGENARIAN. It smuggles vocabulary practice into a branching-logic exercise and makes the output funny enough that kids actually want to test every branch — exactly the behavior you want when teaching conditionals.

The most distinctive choice in the program
Kids running a real software project

The Bat Project wasn't just "build a game together." Three concepts got pitched and one got picked. The group wrote functional requirements. A cave level got mapped out by hand — entrance, quiz gates, obstacles, a monster, an exit. Every component — the stage, the bat, the monster, the quiz — got a named owner. There was a real test phase: unit testing per person, then full integration once everyone's code came together, then launch. That's the actual vocabulary of professional software delivery, handed to a room of kids without simplifying it into something smaller.
